1.1 background 
One of the most serious and sometimes fatal situations that arise in a 
therapeutic setting is tension pneumothorax. Global emergency protocols 
describe it as a form of obstructive shock that happens when air enters the 
pleural space through a "one-way valve" mechanism, either from an open 
chest wound or a lung puncture, but is unable to escape. (Bhangu and others, 
2010). The gradual rise in intrapleural pressure causes complete lung collapse 
and a "mediastinal shift." Venous return and cardiac output are severely 
hampered by this shift, which compresses the heart and major veins (superior 
and inferior vena cava) (World Health Organization, 2018). This illness 
causes cardiovascular collapse and death if it is not treated within minutes.
In emergency nursing, the ability to recognize tension pneumothorax 
clinically is essential. Clinical literature provides evidence that the diagnosis 
is more clinical than radiological. Severe respiratory distress, tachycardia, and 
significant hypotension are important indicators that nurses need to recognize. 
Examining the affected side physically usually reveals asymmetric chest wall 
movement, hyper-resonance to percussion, and reduced or missing breath 
sounds. Although tracheal displacement toward the unaffected side and 
swollen neck veins (JVD) are characteristic symptoms, they are frequently 
late and concerning indicators, thus nurses shouldn't wait for them to manifest 
before starting emergency procedures.(Bhangu et al., 2010].In 2015), 
(Tscheschlog and Jaunch).
Although the WHO and other international organizations have defined 
guidelines for addressing tension pneumothorax, the local hospital 
environment has a significant impact on how these policies are applied. The 3
emergency department during Kirkuk Hospital is the main trauma facility in 
the area. It's critical to comprehend how the nursing staff at this facility 
handles resources, follows management procedures, and makes quick 
interventions. In order to provide the best possible life-saving treatment, this 
study aims to close the gap between local nursing practices at Kirkuk Hospital

Additionally, the educational background of the nursing workforce has a 
significant impact on the efficacy of nurse management. There are forty-five 
nurses working in the emergency room at Kirkuk Hospital. Nonetheless, there 
is a notable difference in educational attainment: 39 nurses (86.7%) are

nurses (13.3%) have a bachelor's degree in nursing. The bulk of the workforce 
may not have the advanced academic and clinical training required for the 
management of high-acuity cases like tension pneumothorax, which presents 
a special obstacle in the application of advanced clinical protocols.
This study fills up important gaps in Kirkuk Hospital's emergency care and 
offers crucial information to the following stakeholders:

2.1 Introduction
 Tension pneumothorax is defined as the accumulation of air under high 
pressure within the pleural cavity. Air enters the pleural space and cannot 
escape, leading to a gradual increase in pressure within the cavity. This 
pressure compresses the lung, shifts the Mediastinum to the opposite side, and 
impedes venous return, significantly reducing cardiac output. According to 
the book “Fundamentals of Medical-Surgical Nursing” (2019), this condition 
requires immediate intervention, beginning with needle aspiration in the 
second intercostal space, followed by the final insertion of a chest tube.
The role of nurses Is crucial in the early recognition of this condition through 
continuous monitoring of vital signs and respiratory status. Nurses must focus 
on detecting early indicators, such as tachycardia and hypotension, to ensure 
the medical team is immediately notified before late complications arise. It is 
important to emphasize that delayed recognition of tracheal displacement can 
have serious consequences, as it is a very late clinical sign indicating a severe 
decline in lung function and an imminent threat of cardiovascular collapse.
2.2 Definition of Pneumothorax and Tension Pneumothorax 
Tension pneumothorax occurs due to the disruption of the parietal, visceral, 
or mediastinal pleura. Lewis et al. (2019) describe it as a “one-way flap valve”
mechanism leading to air entrapment. 8

2.3 Pathophysiology of Tension Pneumothorax
The pleural cavity is normally airless and maintains a slightly negative 
pressure, which facilitates close contact between the pleural layers and allows 
for smooth lung movement during respiration. Tension pneumothorax occurs 
when a one-way valve mechanism is created—whether from an underlying 
lung injury or a chest wall wound—allowing air to enter the pleural space 
during inspiration but preventing its escape during expiration (Hinkle & 
Cheever, 2021).
This continuous, rapid accumulation of air transforms the normal negative 
pressure into progressive positive pressure, which separates the pleural layers 
and leads to:
Lung Collapse: Total compression of the affected lung, resulting in an acute 
ventilation-perfusion mismatch and failure of gas exchange (Hypoxia) (West 
& Luks, 2020). 9
Mediastinal Shift: The mounting intrapleural pressure pushes the mediastinal 
structures (heart, trachea, and major vessels) toward the unaffected side. This 
shift is often clinically manifested by tracheal deviation and distended neck 
veins (Porcel, 2023).
 Hemodynamic Collapse: The mediastinal shift causes significant 
compression of the Vena Cava, severely reducing venous return to the heart. 
This leads to a critical drop in cardiac output and the onset of Obstructive 
Shock, a life-threatening condition that demands immediate recognition by 
the emergency nursing staff and rapid medical intervention (McKnight & 
Burns, 2023).
2.4 Etiology and Risk Factors of Tension Pneumothorax
 Tension pneumothorax arises when air is trapped in the pleural space under 
pressure. Its causes are categorized into three main origins:
2.4.1 Etiology
 Traumatic: The leading cause, resulting from penetrating wounds (e.g., 
stabbings) or blunt trauma (e.g., rib fractures from car accidents) that tear the 
lung tissue (Kasten et al., 2022).
 Iatrogenic: Occurs during medical interventions, such as central line 
insertion, lung biopsies, or high-pressure mechanical ventilation (PEEP) 
(Zarogoulidis et al., 2024).
 Spontaneous: Occurs if a simple pneumothorax (primary or secondary) 
develops a one-way valve mechanism, though this is less common.
2.4.2 Risk Factors
 Mechanical Ventilation: High airway pressures can rupture fragile alveoli, 
especially in ARDS patients. 10
 Chronic Lung Disease: Conditions like COPD, Cystic Fibrosis, and Asthma 
weaken the lung structure (Hinkle & Cheever, 2021).
 Smoking: Increases the formation of subpleural blebs, which are prone to 
rupture. Barotrauma: Rapid pressure changes (e.g., in scuba diving or flying) 
can trigger a collapse (cKnight & Burns, 2023).
2.5Clinical Presentation of Tension Pneumothorax: 
2.5.1 In Patients with Spontaneous Breathing (Awake)
 In the emergency department, the nurse should monitor for a progression 
of symptoms. Initial signs are often respiratory, while late signs indicate 
hemodynamic failure:
 Early signs: Acute onset of shortness of breath (dyspnea), pleuritic chest 
pain, tachypnea, tachycardia, and decreased blood oxygen saturation (SpO2) 
(Brodie & Slutsky, 2011).
 Late (critical) signs: Hypotension (obstructive shock), bradycardia, and 
progressing to cardiac arrest, typically presenting as Pulseless Electrical 
Activity (PEA) (MacDuff et al., 2010).
2.5.2 In Patients on Mechanical Ventilation
 The emergency nurse must maintain high clinical suspicion for TP in 
ventilated patients, as the positive pressure can accelerate lung collapse:
 Sudden and significant drop in oxygen saturation (hypoxia).
 A sudden, rapid increase in peak airway pressure (PIP) on the ventilator 
monitor.
 Rapid hemodynamic collapse, characterized by severe hypotension and 
diminished cardiac output (Roberts et al., 2019).
2.5.3 Physical Examination Findings
 The emergency nursing assessment should focus on the classic triad of 
signs, although they may not all be present simultaneously: 11
Visual Inspection: Asymmetric chest expansion, enlargement, and rigidity on 
the affected side with reduced respiratory movement. Tracheal deviation to 
the contralateral side is a classic but often late sign (ACS, 2018).
Percussion: Hyperresonance (tympanic sound) over the affected side due to 
trapped air.
Auscultation: Absence or significantly diminished breath sounds on the 
affected side (Brodie & Slutsky, 2011).
2.6 Comprehensive Nursing Care Management
 The nursing management of tension pneumothorax is a multi-phase 
process that transitions from life-saving emergency intervention to long-term 
rehabilitative care and education.
2.6.1 Emergency Assessment and Stabilization
 Rapid Clinical Recognition: The nurse must immediately identify the 
“classic triad”: increasing respiratory distress, hypotension, and unilateral 
absence of breath sounds. Positioning: Place the patient in a High-Fowler’s 
position (if hemodynamically stable) to maximize lung expansion and ease 
the work of breathing.
Oxygenation: Administer high-flow oxygen. This not only treats hypoxia 
but also creates a diffusion gradient that accelerates the reabsorption of 
nitrogen from the pleural space (Hinkle & Cheever, 2021).
2.6.2 Assistance with Invasive Procedures
 Needle Decompression Support: Assist the physician in performing 
emergent needle thoracostomy. The nurse is responsible for preparing the 
site with a focus on the 5th intercostal space (anterior to the mid-axillary 
line) as per updated ATLS guidelines, or the 2nd intercostal space (mid 12
clavicular line)and monitoring the patient’s immediate hemodynamic 
response (e.g., rise in blood pressure) after the air hiss is heard.
Chest Tube Thoracostomy: Prepare the sterile field for chest tube insertion. 
The nurse must ensure the Water Seal Drainage system is primed with sterile 
water to the correct level (usually 2cm) before connection (Lewis et al., 
2023).
2.6.3 Maintenance of the Drainage System
 System Integrity: Ensure all connections are taped and secure. The 
drainage unit must always remain upright and below the level of the chest to 
prevent the backflow of fluid or air into the pleural space.
 Monitoring “Tidaling” and “Bubbling”: Tidaling: The nurse observes the 
fluid level rise and fall with respiration. Its absence indicates either lung re-
expansion or a kink in the tubing.
Bubbling: Intermittent bubbling is normal during expiration in a 
pneumothorax patient; however, continuous bubbling in the water-seal 
chamber indicates an external air leak in the system (Baumann et al., 2020).
2.6.4 Post-Procedural Clinical Care
 Respiratory Hygiene: Implement a schedule for coughing and deep 
breathing exercises every 1–2 hours. Use an Incentive Spirometer to 
encourage sustained maximal inspiration, which is vital for re-inflating 
collapsed alveoli (McKnight & Burns, 2023).
Subcutaneous Emphysema Monitoring: Frequently palpate the neck and 
chest for “crepitus” (a crackling sensation under the skin). A rapid increase 
in this sensation must be reported as it suggests the chest tube is 
malpositioned or blocked. 13
Pain Management: Adequate analgesia is not just for comfort; it is a clinical 
necessity. Without it, the patient will “splint” their breathing (taking shallow 
breaths), which increases the risk of atelectasis and pneumonia.
2.6.5 Patient Education and Discharge Planning
 Educate the patient on the signs of recurrence (sudden chest pain or 
shortness of breath). Advise against air travel or SCUBA diving for a period 
(typically several weeks to months) until cleared by a pulmonologist, as 
pressure changes can trigger a recurrence (Hinkle & Cheever, 2021).

2.7 Essential Equipment and Clinical Specifications
 The management of tension pneumothorax requires immediate access to 
specific medical sets. Nurses must ensure the readiness and correct sizing of 
the following equipment:
1. Needle Decompression Set:
 Catheter Size: Large-bore needles, typically 14 to 16 gauge, are required. 14
 Catheter Length: A length of at least 8 cm is recommended to ensure it 
reaches the pleural space through the chest wall, especially in patients with 
high BMI (ATLS, 2018).Clinical Protocol: Ensure readiness for 
decompression at the 5th intercostal space, which is the currently preferred site 
in modern trauma protocols to reduce failure rates.
2. Chest Tube (Thoracostomy) System:
Tube Size: For adults, large-bore chest tubes (28 to 32 French) are typically 
used to drain both air and potential blood (hemothorax).
 Drainage Unit: A three-chamber water-seal suction system (like Pleur-evac) 
is standard. Nurses must monitor the water seal level to maintain negative 
pressure.
3. Skin Preparation and Fixation:
 Antiseptics: Povidone-iodine or Chlorhexidine for site sterilization.
 Sutures: Non-absorbable silk sutures (e.g., 0 or 1-0) to secure the tube in 
place.
 Occlusive Dressing: Petroleum gauze (Vaseline gauze) is essential to create 
an airtight seal around the insertion site to prevent air re-entry.
4. Emergency Monitoring Tools: Continuous Pulse Oximetry (SpO_2) and 
automated blood pressure monitors are critical for the immediate assessment 
of the patient’s hemodynamic response after decompression.
2.8 Challenges in Local Emergency Settings (Kirkuk Hospitals)
 The nursing management of tension pneumothorax in Kirkuk’s teaching 
hospitals faces several practical challenges:
High Trauma Load: The constant influx of road traffic accidents in the 
emergency department (ED) often leads to overcrowding, which may delay 
the rapid triage and recognition of life-threatening symptoms. 15
Resource Limitations: While basic tools are available, the intermittent supply 
of modern drainage systems or varied catheter sizes requires nurses to adapt 
quickly under high-pressure conditions.
 Protocol Updates: A “knowledge-practice gap” exists where some staff may 
still follow older guidelines, highlighting the need for continuous training on 
recent updates like the 5th intercostal space approach (ATLS 10th Ed).
 Communication Barriers: In fast-paced trauma units, the lack of standardized 
tools like SBAR can lead to delays in transmitting critical patient data to the 
surgical team.
 Workload vs. Training: The heavy clinical workload often limits nurses’ 
ability to attend specialized advanced life support (ALS) courses to maintain 
peak competency.
2.9 Previous Studies
 The literature defines tension pneumothorax as a critical emergency caused 
by a ‘one-way flap valve’ mechanism that traps air, leading to rapid lung 
collapse and hemodynamic instability. Studies by West & Luks (2020) and 
McKnight & Burns (2023) emphasize that this condition triggers acute 
hypoxia and obstructive shock due to Vena Cava compression and mediastinal 
shift. Nursing guidelines prioritize the early recognition of the ‘classic triad’ 
(respiratory distress, hypotension, and absent breath sounds) to prevent late 
complications like tracheal deviation. Evidence-based interventions involve 
immediate needle decompression (14-16 gauge) followed by chest tube 
thoracostomy within the ‘safe triangle’ to restore negative pressure. However, 
local research in Kirkuk hospitals highlights a ‘knowledge-practice gap’ 
regarding updated protocols, such as the 5th intercostal space approach, 
alongside resource limitations that may delay life-saving triage. 16
